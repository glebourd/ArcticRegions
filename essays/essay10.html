“Homeward Bound”: Protocols of Truth

As the weather cooled in the last days of September 1869, the Panther and its crew began their journey home. After three months, five thousand miles and numerous notes, sketches and photographs, Bradford now faced the task of crafting summary thoughts on his groundbreaking expedition. Those reflections, and the pictures that accompany them, generate an intriguing twist to final judgments about the issues of photographic truth and geographic accuracy that define The Arctic Regions.

The final chapter forms a microcosm of the book’s rhetorical structure. The text moves from empirical observations of the landscape to fulsome descriptions of it. For instance, after noting the height of mountain climbed just before their final departure, Bradford effused on “the beautiful effects of light and shade as the sunlight played on the multi-formed and parti-colored rocks, relieved by the glistening of the icebergs that frequently intervened” between the ship and Disco Island. In this book, everything ends with a flourish.[1]

This tension between objective and subjective records provides a useful framework for understanding the penultimate photograph of the book. Titled “Homeward Bound,” the image conveys a calming sense of resolution following the final sentence of the volume. The view looks out from the low, dark rock that forms the shore towards the horizon of a tranquil sea.

Yet the meaning of the image depends largely on which particular copy of the book the reader has just finished, because the photographs in some versions have been altered. In some surviving copies of The Arctic Regions, the shaded slabs of some icebergs appear on the distant horizon at the center of the print. When paired with the caption “Homeward Bound,” the picture seems to claim that one final view was made on land before the photographers boarded the ship and set off for Newfoundland. A parting shot, so to speak.

Other versions of the book, however, contradict this narrative. In those copies, a tiny ship appears in front of the central icebergs, with smoke spiraling away from its spindly masts. Close inspection suggests that this ship was either added directly to the negative by hand, or drawn on a print which was itself photographed and then reprinted.

These alterations tangibly change the meaning of the image and by extension the book’s conclusion. Adding the ship reads as a more literal illustration of the phrase “Homeward Bound”; we can now see the ship as it sailed for home. But tying the story up so nicely also complicates things. Are we to pretend the photographers remained behind to document the departure, as the position of the camera implies? Or is it more nuanced, encouraging viewers to stand in the shoes of the Danish and Inuit friends as they watch the Panther sail into the distance? Whatever the intention, the alteration has the unintended consequence of making one question the truth value of the photograph, and by extension the credibility of the images writ large.

Embellishing the photographs like this was a risky move for an artist whose reputation was built on their perceived truthfulness. The London Art Journal, for instance, esteemed that “Mr. Bradford’s are the only works which profess incontrovertible truth in the representation of the northern regions.”[2] In turn, the public believed that paintings like Sermitsialik Glacier (discussed above in the essay Sermitsialik: The Challenges of Scale) were truthful because they copied the composition of specific photographs in The Arctic Regions. Yet, as demonstrated by these conspicuous changes to the prints--the additions of ships to the horizon or polar bears to the empty pack ice--Bradford and his collaborators implied that doctoring “true” photographs made them, perversely, more believable. What explains this willingness to undermine the truth value of their photographs?

On this front, there are no easy answers. There is little documentation of the collaboration between Bradford and staff at Samson and Low, the London-publishing house that printed The Arctic Regions, and nothing that acknowledges the altered photographs. The same is true of James Wallace Black’s photography studio in Boston, where the negatives from the expedition were printed and then shipped to England where they were pasted into each copy of the book. Thus, regarding the artistic intent of altering negatives, there is no smoking gun.

In his essay titled “The Not So Truthful Lens,” Adam Greenhalgh presents a meticulous account of other altered prints in The Arctic Regions to argue that Bradford made what amounts to “a symbolic portrait of a mythical Arctic that had captured the public imagination during the second half of the nineteenth century.”[3] Greenhalgh makes a compelling case that “Bradford’s overall project can be thought of as an attempt to compensate for the limitations he perceived to be inherent to the camera.” Indeed, painters of Bradford’s day often considered photographs crude translations of reality, and that only the interpretive verve of a painter reached the level of “art.” This credible argument has helped to reconcile this historical tension between painting and photography.

Yet to characterize nineteenth-century viewers as foolishly credulous of all photographs is to indulge in other dubious changes, in this case historical. While the neutral, purely mechanistic promise of photography did carry what historians of science Lorraine Daston and Peter Galison have called “an ineradicable glow of veracity,” it was also immediately clear that the medium could be intentionally distorted.[4] That scientists insisted on the truthfulness of photographs in the creations of atlases and theories is unsurprising. But most Victorian viewers approached photographs with a skepticism that is often understated today. As studies by Michael Leja and Jordan Bear have shown, the nineteenth-century public was actually quite discerning in their assessment of photography precisely because they knew how easily it could be manipulated.[5] Indeed, parsing truth from fiction in visual culture became an essential part of developing an education during this period. In this light, Bradford’s pictorial distortions seem to plot a middle path between pictures of ghosts fabricated by spiritualist William H. Mumler and more subtle retouchings by later “straight photographers” like Paul Strand.[6]

Like many of his contemporaries, Bradford recognized the need to correct technical flaws or limitations in early photographic processes in order to make pictures appear more realistic, not less. He made this point in an instructive passage near the end of The Arctic Regions. Below a photograph of a solitary figure on the pack ice in Melville Bay, Bradford wrote “words cannot describe it, neither can the pencil reproduce the grandeur and immensity of the scene, while the camera with all its truthfulness to nature, falls far short.”[7] While black-and-white images like this could not have captured the cool, vivid colors that define the Arctic landscape, they even failed to convey many formal details; the silver iodide used in early collodion negatives registers only low wavelengths of blue and ultraviolet light, yielding high contrast and darkened figures.

In a way, Bradford’s comments offer moral cover to the idea that altering photographs was normal and expected. It’s not that the images were untruthful; it’s that they weren’t true enough. This line of thinking was particularly influential in the years that The Arctic Regions was printed due to the popularity of the photographer Henry Peach Robinson’s 1869 book Pictorial Effect in Photography. In it, the author made a crucial distinction: Truth and fact are not only two words, but, in art at least, they represent two things. A fact is anything done or that exists--a reality. Truth is conformity to that fact or reality--absence of falsehood. So that truth in art may exist without an absolute observance of facts.[8] The book, which Bradford was highly likely to have read and discussed as he finalized production of his own volume, articulated larger protocols of nineteenth-century belief, specifically how representing “truth” was a complex and often conflicted process.

This recognition of ambiguity--of the combination of subjective and objective elements in history--also serves as a fitting coda for a project in the digital humanities. Like Bradford adopted certain scientific codes in his Arctic photographs, the digital humanities has since its inception borrowed various quantification and visualization strategies from the natural and social sciences. As Johanna Drucker argued in her touchstone 2011 article “Humanities Approaches to Graphical Display,” these empirical techniques depend on inherently realist models of knowledge, ones that often resolve in simple and problematic binaries like true or false and black or white. “Rendering observation (the act of creating a statistical, empirical, or subjective account or image) as if it were the same as phenomena observed,” she explains in that text, “collapses the critical distance between the phenomenal world and its interpretation…”[9] Applying her warning to this project, it would be easy to simply categorize Bradford’s images as either geographically accurate or not, or to tally true and false photographs in order to form a summary judgment as to the authenticity of the book on the whole. Instead, I have tried in this project to eschew such binary judgments, to recognize accuracy by degree and to glean insights from deeper truths rather than content myself with historical facts.

To make meaningful contributions not just to scholarly debates but to public knowledge, digital humanities projects like this one can create specific, nuanced models of representation. Protocols for doing so should be continuously written and rewritten in order to live up to the humanistic principle of seeking out, and constantly questioning, where the truth lies.
